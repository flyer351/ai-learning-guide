You've managed people, written work orders, or explained a job to a new hire. Writing a good message to an AI is the same skill. Vague instructions get vague results.

## The recipe: role, task, details, format

- **Role.** Who should it act as? "You're a patient computer teacher." "You're an experienced travel agent."
- **Task.** What exactly do you want? "Write a thank-you note." "Compare these two Medicare plans."
- **Details.** What does it need to know? Names, dates, budget, tone, what you've already tried.
- **Format.** How should the answer look? "Three short paragraphs." "A table." "A checklist I can print."

You don't need all four every time. Even adding one makes a difference.

## Before and after

**Vague:** "Write a letter to my HOA."

**Better:** "Write a polite but firm letter to my homeowners association. Our street light has been out for six weeks and I reported it on March 3 and April 10 with no reply. Ask for a repair date within two weeks. Keep it under 200 words."

The second version takes 30 seconds longer to write and saves you three rounds of edits.

<div class="try">

**Try it now.** Pick one of the tasks you jotted down in lesson 1 and write a message using the recipe. Or adapt this one:

> You're a friendly gardening expert. I live in central Missouri and have a sunny 4-by-8 foot raised bed. Suggest five vegetables that are easy for a beginner, with when to plant each. Put it in a simple table.

</div>

## When the answer misses

Don't start over. Steer it, the same way you'd give feedback to a colleague:

- "Shorter, please."
- "More formal. This is going to the bank."
- "You assumed I have a garage. I don't."
- "Ask me any questions you need before you try again."

That last one is a favorite. It turns the AI into an interviewer and often leads to the best results.

<div class="takeaway">

**The takeaway.** Treat it like a capable new assistant: give context, be specific, and give feedback.
